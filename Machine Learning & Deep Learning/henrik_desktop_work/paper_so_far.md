1.0 Introduction
Wildfires continue to pose a significant risk to society and ecological systems, causing substantial economic losses, environmental damage, and threats to human life. Recent studies indicate a global increase in the intensity, severity, and geographic spread of extreme weather events, including wildfires, which displace over 25 million people annually (NASA, 2024; Kalina Velev, 2025; IDMC, 2022). Furthermore, wildfires contribute to a reinforcing feedback loop by accelerating climate change, thereby exacerbating the conditions that drive their occurrence.
To mitigate this growing threat, it is essential to implement effective prevention and response strategies. One promising approach is using Machine Learning (ML) for early detection, which can help reduce the scale of damage and improve response times. Therefore, we explore how to develop a classification model that analyses satellite imagery to detect smoke. We chose satellite imagery because it enables efficient monitoring of large geographic areas, offering broader coverage than the surveillance footage typically used for fire detection (Ba et al., 2019). Smoke detection is relevant because smoke is often the earliest visible indicator of wildfire ignition and can pose serious health risks to humans when inhaled. We chose to use a dataset that includes a variety of weather phenomena that could be mistaken for smoke. This allows us to address the challenges of accurately classifying smoke in satellite imagery, where visual similarities and varying natural backgrounds can complicate detection and affect model performance. 
Therefore, our research question for this paper is: To what extent can a machine learning architecture effectively classify wildfire smoke patterns in the presence of interfering variables, such as diverse meteorological conditions and terrestrial backgrounds?
In this paper, we compare four models: two custom Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), a fine-tuned ResNet50, and a Support Vector Machine (SVM) baseline, to determine which approach achieves the highest accuracy. To support this analysis, Section 2 reviews related work, providing a foundation for our approach. Building on this, Section 3 outlines the dataset preparation and preprocessing pipeline. We then present and compare the models' performance in Section 5, before moving to Section 6, where we discuss the key findings, limitations, and potential directions for future improvement.

2.0 Related work
Fire and smoke detection using machine learning is a well-established area of research, with approaches developed across a range of sensing platforms, including ground-based surveillance systems, drones, and satellite imagery (Yar et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Ba et al., 2019). The objective of this research stream is to advance early fire detection capabilities, thereby reducing the environmental and economic losses associated with large-scale wildfires. 
Wang et al. (2024) address a key limitation in the field by introducing the large-scale FASDD dataset, which contains over 120,000 annotated instances across different sensing platforms. Their work highlights that a major challenge in current research is the lack of sufficiently large and diverse datasets, which hinders models' ability to generalise across different scenarios. They demonstrate that transformer-based models benefit significantly from pretraining on heterogeneous data, leading to improved performance across domains. However, emphasising that variability in image scale, resolution, and feature distribution, particularly in satellite imagery, remains a significant challenge for robust detection.
Similarly, Muhammad et al. (2023) extend this line of research by investigating fire detection under adverse weather conditions such as fog, haze, and low-light environments. They propose an attention-based convolutional neural network designed to focus on relevant fire-related features while suppressing noise introduced by environmental interference. This work highlights a critical gap in existing research, namely that many models are trained under controlled conditions and therefore do not adequately account for real-world variability and confounding environmental factors. To address the limited availability of real-world data under such conditions, the authors employ data augmentation techniques to simulate diverse weather scenarios. Their results demonstrate improved robustness compared to standard CNN architectures. However, the reliance on synthetic data remains a limitation, as it may not fully capture the complexity and unpredictability of real-world environments.
Research that addresses this gap includes Ba et al. (2019), which focuses on satellite-based smoke detection. They propose SmokeNet, a convolutional neural network that incorporates both spatial and channel-wise attention mechanisms to improve feature extraction. Their approach addresses the challenge that smoke often appears visually similar to clouds, haze, and dust in satellite images. To support this, they introduce the USTC_SmokeRS dataset, which includes multiple confusing classes to better reflect real-world conditions. While their model achieves high classification accuracy, the approach is limited by the relatively small dataset size and its focus on scene-level classification rather than precise localisation of smoke regions.

4 Methodology
4.1 Dataset Description
The models created in this project are trained on the USTC_SmokeRS dataset, introduced by Ba et al. (2019) for the development of SmokeNet. To enable differentiation between smoke and visually similar natural phenomena, the dataset includes six wildfire-related scene classes: Cloud, Dust, Haze, Land, Seaside, and Smoke. The dataset is based on Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) data and includes various natural backgrounds captured over the span of almost twenty years, covering every continent except Antarctica (Ba et al., 2019.) 

The dataset is structured with two features per row: the image itself and its associated class label. It contains 6,225 RGB satellite images that have an approximately balanced distribution across the classes, with each category containing between 1,002 and 1,164 images as shown in Figure X. Each image has a resolution of 256 × 256 pixels and is stored in uint8 format. 4.2 Data Pre-Processing
4.2.1 Data Filtering


To ensure the integrity of the dataset prior to model training, four independent quality checks were applied across all 6,225 images. The filtering pipeline is illustrated in Figure X. All checks follow a flag-and-report policy,  no images are removed from the dataset regardless of findings, as the dataset is sourced from a curated public benchmark and removal could compromise the reproducibility of results.
Two complementary hash-based methods were employed to detect duplicate images. The first is a custom average hash (aHash): each image is converted to grayscale, downsampled to 12×12 pixels, and each pixel is compared to the image mean to produce a binary fingerprint. The fingerprint is packed into a single integer, and images sharing an identical hash are flagged as duplicates. This method is computationally lightweight but only detects near-identical images. Across all 6,225 images, aHash flagged one image (idx 5196) as a potential duplicate of idx 5178.

To cross-validate this finding, a second pass was performed using perceptual hashing (pHash) from the imagehash library. Unlike aHash, pHash applies a Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) over the image's frequency content, making it more robust to minor variations in brightness and contrast. pHash returned zero duplicates across the full dataset. Manual visual inspection of the aHash-flagged pair (Figure X) confirmed that the two images are not true duplicates, but rather satellite captures of the same geographical area from slightly different vantage points. No images were removed.
To check for corrupt images each image was loaded and converted to a NumPy array, then validated against two structural conditions: the array must have exactly three dimensions (H × W × C), and the channel count must equal three (RGB). Any image failing these assertions or raising an exception during loading was flagged as corrupt. No corrupt or unreadable images were identified in the dataset.
To detect RGB anomalies the global mean pixel value was computed for each image. Images with a mean below 5 or above 250 were flagged as potential anomalies, targeting near-black images (underexposed or blacked-out) and near-white images (saturated or blank). No anomalies were detected.

4.2.2 Data Normilisation
Normalisation is applied after resizing and prior to model training to ensure consistent pixel value distributions across all splits. Two schemes are defined, each tailored to the requirements of the models that use them, and summarised in Table X.

Min-Max scaling [0, 1] was used for the Custom CNN and SVM. Pixel values was rescaled by dividing each uint8 value by 255, mapping the original range [0, 255] to float32 [0.0, 1.0]. This is a fixed, dataset-agnostic transform requiring no per-channel statistics.
For the ResNet50 transfer learning model ImageNet standardisation was used. The pixel values was first scaled to [0, 1], then standardised using the per-channel ImageNet mean and standard deviation (Table X). This matches the input distribution the ResNet50 backbone was pre-trained on, which is necessary for meaningful feature transfer.
Both schemes are applied through a shared utility function load_split(df, normalise_fn), which accepts either normalisation function and builds the full array for a given split. This produces six arrays in total, X_train, X_val, and X_test under both schemes, ensuring that the validation and test sets are always processed with the same function as the training set, preventing data leakage.
Additionaly another standardisation was used within the SVM pipeline, a StandardScaler (zero mean, unit variance) fitted exclusively on the training set and applied after PCA dimensionality reduction. Both steps are encapsulated in a scikit-learn Pipeline, ensuring no data leakage between splits.
4.2.3 Data Augmentation


Augmentation is applied exclusively to the training set. The validation and test sets are never augmented, ensuring unbiased evaluation. The purpose is to expand the effective training set size and improve generalisation by exposing the models to realistic variations in the satellite imagery.
Four augmentation types are defined at the PIL level and shared across models: horizontal flipping, random rotation (90°, 180°, or 270°), random brightness adjustment with a factor sampled uniformly from [0.5, 2.0], and Gaussian noise injection with mean 0 and standard deviation 10 applied to the raw [0, 255] pixel values before clipping back to a valid range. These transforms reflect realistic variation in remote sensing imagery, different viewing angles, lighting conditions, and sensor noise, making them relevant this task and for making a more generalizable model. 
Rather than applying augmentation randomly on-the-fly, the training DataFrame is expanded deterministically into five copies: one original and one per augmentation type. This expands the training set from 3,735 to 18,675 images (5×), with each row carrying an augmentation code that specifies which transform to apply when the image is loaded. Class proportions are preserved across all five copies.
The SVM uses the original non-augmented training set directly, as the high dimensionality of flattened 224×224 images (150,528 features per image) means the model already has sufficient information to learn class boundaries without additional copies of the training data. The Custom CNN and ResNet50 both apply the same four transforms via a TensorFlow tf.data pipeline that applies augmentation randomly per batch during training rather than as fixed copies, providing greater variety across epochs. For the CNN, a final clip to [0, 1] is applied after noise injection to preserve the normalised range. For ResNet50, this clip is omitted since ImageNet-normalised values can legitimately be negative.

5 Model Framework
This project includes four models. The development of a custom convolutional neural network (CNN) began with CNN Model 1 (M1), which is based on the Visual Geometry Group (VGG) architecture. Building on this foundation, a second custom CNN model (M2) was developed through iterative refinement of the network architecture. To benchmark the performance of the custom models, two additional approaches were implemented: a pre-trained ResNet50 model and a support vector machine (SVM).

5.1 Custom CNNs
We chose to use a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) to effectively classify whether a satellite image represents a fire-related situation, such as ground truth smoke detection or a natural weather condition. A CNN was selected because they are widely used and highly effective for visual recognition tasks, particularly image classification (Géron, 2017/2019, Chapter 14). 
The initial CNN model is a three-block convolutional network designed from scratch. Each block follows the same internal structure, with filter counts doubling across blocks from 32 to 128, and spatial dimensions reducing from 224×224 at input to 28×28 after the third block. A GlobalAveragePooling2D layer connects the convolutional base to a one-layer dense head with 256 neurons before the final six-neuron softmax output.   
All convolutions use 3×3 kernels with identical padding, meaning spatial reduction only occurs at the explicit MaxPool steps. Two convolution layers are stacked within each block before pooling, following the VGG pattern (Simonyan & Zisserman, 2014), where two 3×3 convolutions cover the same receptive field as a single 5×5 convolution but with fewer parameters and an additional non-linearity. Doubling the filter count at each block reflects the standard CNN design principle that early layers detect simple low-level features such as edges and textures, while deeper layers require more filters to represent the complex, abstract patterns needed to distinguish between visually similar classes such as smoke, haze, and dust.
BatchNormalisation is applied after every Conv2D layer to stabilise the input distribution to each layer, reduce internal covariate shift, and allow higher learning rates without divergence (Ioffe & Szegedy, 2015). Dropout (0.25) is applied after each convolutional block, a mild rate appropriate for convolutional layers, which already benefit from implicit regularisation through weight sharing. A higher dropout rate of 0.5 is applied to the dense layer, where the risk of overfitting is greater.
Rather than flattening the 28×28×128 feature maps, which would produce 100,352 inputs to the dense head and risk severe overfitting, GlobalAveragePooling2D is used to reduce each feature map to a single value, yielding a 128-dimensional representation. This also preserves the spatial structure needed for Grad-CAM visualisations. 
Following training and error analysis of the initial CNN, the model was iteratively refined to improve performance. The second CNN builds on the first as a baseline, incorporating several architectural and training enhancements.
Architecturally, the model retains the original convolutional blocks but introduces a fourth block with 256 filters, further reducing the spatial dimensions to 14 × 14. In the dense head, an additional fully connected layer with 512 neurons and a dropout rate of 0.4 is included, enabling more gradual feature compression and the learning of richer feature representations prior to the final classification. The full architecture is detailed in Appendix X, Figure X.
The training procedure largely follows that of the initial model, using the AdamW optimizer (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10⁻³. However, the learning rate schedule is modified to include a 5-epoch linear warmup followed by cosine decay over a maximum of 100 epochs. The warmup phase mitigates unstable gradient updates in early training.
To improve generalization, label smoothing with a factor of 0.15 is applied to the cross-entropy loss, reducing overconfident predictions, particularly important given the visual similarity between haze, smoke, and dust. Class weights are further incorporated to penalize misclassification of the more challenging classes (haze and smoke) while reducing the influence of the most visually distinct class (seaside). Finally, early stopping with a patience of 10 epochs is employed, monitoring validation accuracy and restoring the best-performing weights. This allows sufficient training time for the cosine decay schedule to yield improvements at lower learning rates.

4.3.2 ResNet50 - Transfer Learning
The second model applies transfer learning using ResNet50, a 50-layer convolutional neural network pretrained on ImageNet (1.2 million images, 1,000 classes), and serves as a strong baseline to evaluate the performance of the custom CNN against. Rather than training a deep network from scratch on our relatively small dataset of 3,735 training images, transfer learning reuses the feature representations already learned by ResNet50, including edges, textures, shapes, and increasingly abstract visual patterns, and adapts them to our six-class satellite classification task.
The ResNet50 backbone is loaded with pretrained ImageNet weights, with the original 1,000-class classification head removed. The backbone produces a 7×7×2048 feature map, which is reduced to a 2,048-dimensional vector via GlobalAveragePooling2D, chosen over flattening which would produce 100,352 inputs to the dense head and introduce substantial overfitting risk. A lightweight classification head consisting of a Dense(256) layer with BatchNormalisation, ReLU activation, and Dropout(0.5) is added, followed by a six-neuron softmax output layer. ResNet50's defining feature is its residual connections, skip connections that allow gradients to flow directly past convolutional blocks.
Training follows a two-phase strategy to avoid destroying the pretrained weights before the new head has stabilised. In Phase 1, all backbone layers are frozen and only the classification head is trained using Adam at a learning rate of 1×10⁻³ for up to 150 epochs with EarlyStopping (patience=5). In Phase 2, the last approximately 30 layers of the backbone (Stage 5 and the end of Stage 4) are unfrozen and trained jointly with the head at a much lower learning rate of 1×10⁻⁵. The drastically reduced learning rate is necessary to nudge the pretrained weights toward our domain without catastrophic forgetting of the general visual features learned on ImageNet. Early layers, which capture universal low-level features such as edges and colours that transfer well across domains, remain frozen throughout. ReduceLROnPlateau (factor=0.5, patience=3) is applied in Phase 2 to halve the learning rate if validation loss stalls.
ResNet50 was chosen because it is one of the most well-studied architectures in the literature, making results straightforward to contextualise. Its residual learning mechanism provides a clean theoretical motivation for its use as a transfer learning backbone, and its size is well-suited to our dataset, large enough to capture complex visual patterns but not so large as to be impractical to fine-tune.

SVM - Baseline
As a non-neural baseline, we implement a support vector machine (SVM) model for comparison with our neural network approaches. Each input image is first flattened into a 150,528-dimensional feature vector. To reduce dimensionality, we apply principal component analysis (PCA) with 200 components, retaining 94.2% of the total variance. 
The resulting pipeline consists of PCA followed by a StandardScaler, which normalizes each component to zero mean and unit variance, ensuring equal weighting across features. Since SVM is inherently a binary classifier, a multi-class strategy is required. We adopt a one-vs-rest (OvR) approach, where one classifier is trained per class, and the final prediction is determined by the classifier with the highest decision score. 
Hyperparameter tuning is subsequently performed through grid search to identify the optimal configuration. Evaluation Metrics
To evaluate the performance of our models, we consider three metrics: precision, recall, and the F1 score. Precision measures the proportion of predicted positive instances that are correct, while recall (also referred to as the true positive rate) quantifies the proportion of actual positive instances that are correctly identified. The F1 score represents the harmonic mean of precision and recall, providing a balanced measure of both.These metrics are computed for all six classes. However, since the primary objective of this project is the detection of smoke, particular emphasis is placed on the performance of the smoke class.
Given the inherent trade-off between precision and recall, we prioritize recall in our evaluation. In this context, it is more critical to identify as many instances of smoke as possible, even at the expense of an increased number of false positives. Missing a true smoke instance (false negative) is considered more severe than incorrectly classifying a non-smoke instance as smoke (false positive), as the latter can be further verified, whereas the former represents a failure to detect a potential fire event.

A likely explanation for this pattern lies in the visual nature of the classification task. Smoke, haze, and dust are all atmospheric phenomena that share overlapping visual characteristics in satellite imagery, such as diffuse colour gradients, semi-transparency, and irregular boundaries. Critically, these phenomena do not appear in isolation, they are observed over varying terrain, including both land and seaside backgrounds. This means the model must simultaneously disentangle the atmospheric phenomenon from the underlying terrain, which introduces an additional source of visual ambiguity that is not present in classification tasks with uniform backgrounds. The tendency to over-predict seaside is consistent with this explanation, as seaside is a visually distinctive and easy-to-learn class that the model learns to rely on as a safe fallback when the atmospheric signal is ambiguous (Ba et al., 2019). These observed failure modes directly motivated the class weight intervention introduced in M2, which significantly increased both model accuracy and recall on smoke.
